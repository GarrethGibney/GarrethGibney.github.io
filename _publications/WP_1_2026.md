## Abstract

This paper examines the short-run relationship between heat exposure and labor conflict in the United States. We find that high temperatures increase labor conflict, with effects that grow disproportionately as heat exposure accumulates. According to our results, 2.7% of labor conflict events in summer months can be attributed to heat exposure. We also show that labor protests are more sensitive to heat exposure than strikes and that effects are concentrated in the transportation and healthcare sectors. We conclude that heat is a catalyst for labor conflict, whose influence could grow as the frequency of high temperatures increases with climate change.
